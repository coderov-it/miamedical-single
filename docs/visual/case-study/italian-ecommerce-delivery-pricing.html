How Italian e-commerce actually prices delivery

Before building a five-level geographic pricing tree, the obvious question: what do comparable Italian businesses do? The short answer is far less than we were planning — and the closest analogues to this business do almost nothing at all.

Method and limits. Desk research on public delivery pages, read August 2026, weighted towards medical-aid rental as the nearest vertical, plus the shipping-zone data models of the two platforms most Italian shops run on. Nobody was interviewed. Two sources would not disclose: Mondo Convenienza's cost page truncated on fetch, and sanitaria.it publishes "request a quote" only. Web search was US-region, which under-samples small Italian operators. Individual prices will drift — the pattern is the finding, not the figures.

Three things the evidence says

0 of 4 Direct competitors price by location Every medical-aid rental operator found charges a flat fee, folds it into the day rate, or quotes by phone. Two of them charge exactly €25.
Gate What CAP is actually used for Esselunga asks for your CAP to answer "do we serve you?" — a yes/no, not a price. That is the industry's main use of postcodes.
What, not where The real price driver Order value, weight, product category, assembly. Geography enters as a surcharge for islands and awkward cities, never as a national gradient.

Nobody found builds anything like our tree

Not one of these companies models region → province → comune → CAP → frazione with inherited prices. The most geographic any of them gets is IKEA's island surcharge list and a couple of named city zones. Our design is more machinery than any comparable Italian business runs on — including the ones that own their own delivery fleets and deliver furniture to mountain villages.

What the platforms model

Most Italian shops run on WooCommerce or Shopify. Both solved this problem years ago, and both landed on the same shape — and it is not a hierarchy.

WooCommerce

A flat, manually ordered list of zones. Each zone holds regions plus an optional postcode list, which accepts exact codes and prefix wildcards (902*). At checkout, zones are tested top to bottom and the first match wins — one zone per customer, no exceptions. Specificity is not computed; the shop owner drags the specific zones above the broad ones.

Shopify

The same model: ordered custom zones, first match wins, postcodes as exact values, ranges or wildcards. Their own guidance is to prefer ranges and wildcards over enumerating codes. Notably, native Shopify zones stop at region level — postcode and city precision needs an app, which tells you how rarely shops need it.

The one thing both platforms have that we do not

An explicit order, set by hand. No inheritance, no computed specificity ranking, no tree to reason about. The owner decides what beats what by dragging rows. That is a weaker model on paper and a much easier one to hold in your head — and it is what thousands of Italian shops actually operate.

The tree's one real advantage survives, for one row

Inheritance existed to guarantee no gaps — an address always finds a price. The ordered list gets the same guarantee from a single catch-all zone at the bottom. So the whole reason for five levels and a nullable inherit state collapses into one row that says "everything else: phone quote".

What I would change

  1. Ask the client the question this research raises first: their competitors charge a flat €25. Do they actually want location pricing, or did they hand over a spreadsheet because I asked for one? This is a commercial decision worth an explicit answer before any more schema.
  2. If they confirm location pricing: replace the five-level tree with an ordered zone list — one table, a priority integer, and match rules on comune codes and/or CAPs. Keep the needs_call state; drop inheritance and the specificity ranking.
  3. Drop the frazione level for now. No company found prices below CAP, and the match data was never reliable. It can be a match rule on a zone later if it is ever needed.
  4. Keep istat_comuni. It is still how a provider's comune name becomes a stable code, whichever pricing shape wins.
  5. Borrow two things nobody asked for but the peers all have: free store pickup — which we already have — and Esselunga's reduced or free delivery for disabled customers, which for a medical-equipment business is close to an expectation.

What this does not change

The reasoning about geography still holds: comune is the safe key, CAP is not a unique key, and a shared CAP must be matched together with its comune. The industry model handles all three — it just does it with a flat priority list instead of a hierarchy. Nothing learned earlier was wrong; it was applied to more levels than the problem needs.
